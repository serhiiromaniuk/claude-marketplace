Executive Summary

Kestrelbox runs 1,140 parcel lockers on a single cloud platform, built and shipped from one Git organisation. The service works and customers rarely see a failure, but the review turned up 20 findings (1 critical, 6 high), and the worst of them sit in the fleet, not the cloud. Every locker trusts the same maintenance password and accepts unsigned firmware, and the whole fleet talks through one broker replica. For budget-holders the message is short: about 12.5 engineer-days of work, and the run-cost goes down, because a committed-use discount more than pays for the added redundancy. The pattern is not "badly built" but "built for 200 lockers, now running 1,140."

Architecture — One Fleet, One Platform, One Toolchain

Kestrelbox has three estates that depend on each other in one direction. Lockers hold an mTLS session to an MQTT broker on the cloud platform over LTE; the platform runs the booking and locker APIs; the Git organisation builds both the platform and the locker firmware, which reaches the fleet through a storage bucket. The weakest links are not in the busiest component — they are in the update path and the shared maintenance credential.

Lockers connect to the broker over LTE with mutual TLS. CI deploys the platform with short-lived OIDC credentials, but firmware images travel through a storage bucket to the fleet with only a CRC check.
One key, many doors

The service-port password is the same on all 1,140 lockers and is printed in the technician guide. Its secrecy is only as good as the least careful copy of that guide.

The update path is the attack path

Whoever can write to the firmware bucket can install code on every locker. The platform deploy path is strong; this one is not.

One broker, one queue

Every door release passes through a single broker pod. A routine node upgrade is enough to strand parcels across the network for several minutes.

The cloud platform is the part of Kestrelbox that looks most complex — and it is the part in the best shape. The risk sits at the edges: in a password and an update channel.

Cost Actuals & Backup / Recovery Posture

Platform spend · billing export, verified

Platform spend is ≈ $6,480 a month (about $78k a year), down from $6,910 in June after a log-retention change. Fleet connectivity adds ≈ $2,170 / month in LTE data plans (1,140 × $1.90).

Read: steady compute that runs flat all month dominates the bill — exactly what a committed-use discount is for (P7, ≈ $520/mo). Broker replicas and PITR storage add ≈ $150/mo, so the remediation lowers the bill by ≈ $370/mo.

  • Regional HA protects the database from a zone failure — not from a bad migration or a deleted table
  • Nightly backups run at 02:00 UTC; anything after that is lost in a restore (P2)
  • Lockers keep releasing parcels offline, so a short broker outage is slow, not lost (E3)
  • Firmware bucket versioning means a bad image can be rolled back in minutes

Recovery bottom line. Kestrelbox is protected against the failures it has already had — a zone outage, a broker restart — and exposed to the one it hasn't: a logical error in the bookings data. Turning on PITR and running one restore drill (≈ half a day, in P2) closes that gap and gives the RTO a measured value.
The platform's weak point is not what it spends — it is that nobody has yet proven a restore. Half a day of rehearsal turns an estimate into a fact.

Impact & Business Case

The work takes few hours, and the losses it prevents are lopsided: most high findings guard against rare, expensive events (fleet-wide access, a tampered rollout, a lost day of bookings), and two guard against frequent, cheap ones (stalled releases, faulty doors left in service). Prioritised Roadmap

 Wave 1 — Quick wins ~5.25 d

High impact · low effort · reversible

  • E1 Per-locker credentials
  • P1 Private Kubernetes API
  • D1 + D3 Require 2FA; automate leavers
  • P2 PITR + one restore drill
  • P4 Workload identity, revoke keys
  • E4 + P5 Certificate renewal job; paging

 Wave 2 — Strategic ~5.5 d

High impact · needs a window

  • E2 Signed firmware (v4 / v5 first)
  • E3 Three broker replicas, spread across zones
  • E5 Actuator-fault alerting
  • P3 Planned node-pool upgrade
  • D4 + D5 Terraform import; dependency triage

 Wave 3 — Hygiene ~1.75 d

Low effort · opportunistic

  • P7 Buy the committed-use discount
  • P6 Requests & limits on every deployment
  • D2 Remove the admin bypass
  • P8 / D6 Chart refresh; runbooks to the wiki
Bottom line. Kestrelbox's platform is sound where customers look and thin where attackers look. About two and a half engineer-weeks — front-loaded with Wave 1, which removes the critical finding and four of the six highs — lifts the estate from "C / Fair" toward "B / Good", and the committed-use discount makes the bill smaller, not bigger. One decision is strategic rather than technical: retiring the v3 locker controllers (E6) so the whole fleet can take signed firmware.
Twelve and a half engineer-days, and a smaller monthly bill, stand between "C / Fair" and "B / Good." The asymmetry is the headline.
