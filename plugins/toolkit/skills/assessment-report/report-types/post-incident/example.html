Executive Summary

At 14:11 on 04 July, a routine Aurora PostgreSQL minor-version patch triggered an unexpected writer failover on the checkout database. The failover itself lasted only ~30 seconds — a healthy service should have ridden it out. Instead, the checkout service's connection pool exhausted with no backpressure, and an aggressive, un-capped retry policy with no circuit breaker turned the brief blip into a self-sustaining retry storm. Roughly 18% of checkout attempts failed (peaking near 34%) until retries were throttled and pools recycled at 15:07, with full recovery by 15:41. The patch was the trigger; the root cause was the missing backpressure and retry guardrails. The fix is small and cheap; the avoided repeat is not.

Root Cause Analysis

The causal chain below traces the incident from trigger to effect. The trigger (the patch-induced failover) was routine and expected to be survivable; the root cause is the pair of missing guardrails in the checkout service that let a brief dependency blip become a self-sustaining overload.

Trigger, not cause

The patch and its failover were routine and short-lived. Blaming the patch would fix nothing — the next failover (planned or not) would cascade the same way.

The real cause

Checkout lacked two standard guardrails: pool backpressure (bound the wait, fail fast) and a circuit breaker with capped, jittered retries. Together they would have contained the blip.

Why it self-sustained

Immediate un-capped retries meant the recovered writer was hit with a backlog larger than steady-state — the system kept knocking itself down until retries were throttled by hand.

Root cause, stated plainly. A brief, survivable database failover became a 45-minute outage because the checkout service had no connection-pool backpressure and no circuit breaker on an aggressive retry path. This is a systemic design gap, present long before 04 July and independent of who was on call. The remediation (AI-01/02/03) targets exactly this gap.

What Went Well / What Went Poorly

A blameless review names both. The response, once engaged, was fast and correct — that is worth stating as plainly as the gaps.

 What went well keep doing

  • Fast acknowledgement — MTTA ~2 min once the alert fired; on-call engaged immediately.
  • Correct diagnosis under pressure — the team distinguished "DB down" from "retry storm sustaining load" (14:52) rather than chasing the database.
  • Decisive, low-risk mitigation — throttle + pool recycle + load shed; no risky failback attempts.
  • Blast radius stayed contained — service isolation held; catalog, cart, search, account were unaffected.
  • Clear comms — status page updated at escalation; internal channel kept a clean decision log.
  • No data loss — carts persisted; the incident was fully reversible.

 What went poorly fix

  • Detection lag ~8 min — the loose alert threshold (CF-P1) let the storm establish before paging.
  • No guardrails — the absence of backpressure + circuit breaker (CF-T1/T2) is what made the incident possible at all.
  • Stale runbook — the opened runbook (CF-P2) was unusable; diagnosis fell back to raw telemetry.
  • Business-hours patch, no canary — a survivable failover landed at peak traffic (CF-P3).
  • Escalation hesitancy — ~7 min at SEV2 before SEV1 (CF-O2); criteria were ambiguous.
  • Unowned resilience path — the guardrail gap had no backlog home (CF-O1).
The system worked as built

Isolation, comms and mitigation all performed. The failures were missing capabilities, not botched execution.

Diagnosis was the hard part

Recognising the DB had already recovered — and the app was hurting itself — is a genuinely non-obvious call the team got right.
